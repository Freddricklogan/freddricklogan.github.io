What internationalization actually means

Internationalization is not a single program or a study-abroad office. Jane Knight's widely adopted definition frames it as a process woven through the core of the institution.

Knight's definition

A process, not a product

Internationalization is "the process of integrating an international, intercultural, or global dimension into the purpose, functions, or delivery of post-secondary education" — that is, into teaching, research, and service. It is a means to institutional goals, not an end in itself.

Key nuance: Knight distinguishes internationalization from globalization — globalization is the flow of ideas, people, and capital across borders (a context); internationalization is how a nation or institution deliberately responds to it.

At home vs. abroad

Two complementary streams

DimensionAt homeAbroad / cross-border
LocusOn the home campusAcross national borders
ReachesAll students, incl. non-mobileMobile students, staff, programs
LeversCurriculum, COIL, campus diversityMobility, branch campuses, partnerships
Equity noteDemocratizes global learningDepends on resources & access
Comprehensive internationalization (John Hudzik / ACE) is the commitment to infuse international and comparative perspectives throughout the entire institution — mission, leadership, curriculum, faculty, and partnerships — rather than treating it as a set of isolated activities. "Internationalization at home" (Beelen & Jones) ensures the 90%+ of students who never travel still gain intercultural and global competence.
Drivers & dangers

Rationales for going global — and the risks

Knight and de Wit group the motivations for internationalization into four families. Naming your dominant rationale clarifies strategy — and exposes the trade-offs each one carries.

Academic

Quality & reputation

Enhancing academic standards, research capacity, curriculum, and institutional profile through global engagement and benchmarking.

Economic

Revenue & competitiveness

Tuition income from international students, workforce and skills development, and national economic competitiveness.

Political

Diplomacy & influence

Foreign policy, soft power, national security, and strategic ties — education as an instrument of international relations.

Social / cultural

Understanding & citizenship

Intercultural understanding, global citizenship, national cultural identity, and preparing graduates for a connected world.

Hans de Wit and colleagues warn against "internationalization of higher education" becoming a checklist of activities detached from purpose. The corrective is a values-driven, inclusive vision — de Wit's updated definition adds that it should be "intentional" and aimed at enhancing quality and making a "meaningful contribution to society."

Internationalization strategy explorer

Comprehensive internationalization is assembled from distinct levers. Select a lever to see its definition, the benefits it delivers, and the challenges leaders must manage. No single lever is a strategy on its own.

Student & scholar mobility

The physical movement of students, faculty, and staff across borders for study, teaching, or research — the most visible and traditional lever, spanning short-term study abroad, credit mobility, and full degree mobility.

Definition: inbound (hosting international students) and outbound (sending home students abroad) flows, credit vs. degree mobility.

  • BenefitsIntercultural competence, language gains, employability, campus diversity, tuition revenue
  • ChallengesCost & equity of access, health/safety & risk, credit transfer, visa dependence, re-entry support
  • ReachesA minority of students — hence "at home" complements it
  • Owned byStudy abroad & international student offices

International branch campuses

An institution establishes a physical presence in another country, awarding its own credential in the host location — a form of program and provider mobility that moves the institution to the student.

Definition: cross-border delivery where the awarding institution operates a campus abroad (e.g. education hubs in the Gulf, Southeast Asia, China).

  • BenefitsAccess for non-mobile students, global brand, new markets, host-country capacity building
  • ChallengesQuality assurance across jurisdictions, academic freedom, financial risk, cultural & legal fit
  • ModelWholly owned, joint venture, or government-partnered
  • GovernanceHome & host accreditation both apply

Partnerships & joint / dual degrees

Formal agreements between institutions in different countries — from student exchange and articulation to joint degrees (one shared credential) and dual/double degrees (two credentials) delivered collaboratively.

Definition: collaborative programs, consortia, and Memoranda of Understanding that pool curricula, faculty, and recognition.

  • BenefitsShared expertise, recruitment pipelines, cost-sharing, mutual recognition of credit
  • ChallengesCredit & degree recognition, quality alignment, sustainability, unequal partner power
  • SpectrumMOU → exchange → articulation → joint/dual degree
  • Success factorReciprocity & clear governance, not one-way flows

Internationalized curriculum

Embedding international, intercultural, and global perspectives into the content, learning outcomes, and teaching of programs — the heart of "internationalization at home," reaching every student regardless of mobility.

Definition: Internationalization of the Curriculum (Betty Leask) — intentional design so all graduates develop global and intercultural capabilities.

  • BenefitsEquitable global learning, employability, inclusive of non-mobile & local students
  • ChallengesFaculty buy-in & development, avoiding tokenism, assessing intercultural outcomes
  • ScopeContent, pedagogy, languages, and hidden curriculum
  • Owned byFaculty & academic units, not just the IO

International research collaboration

Cross-border co-authorship, joint grants, shared infrastructure, and researcher mobility that address problems no single nation can solve alone — increasingly the engine of global knowledge production.

Definition: collaborative research networks, international co-publication, and mobility of scholars and doctoral candidates.

  • BenefitsHigher impact & citation, access to funding & facilities, capacity building, global challenges
  • ChallengesEquitable authorship, research security & ethics, IP, "helicopter/parachute" research
  • TrendRising share of internationally co-authored papers
  • EthicsFair credit & benefit-sharing with Global South partners

Virtual exchange / COIL

Collaborative Online International Learning connects classes in different countries through technology-enabled, sustained collaboration — delivering intercultural learning without the cost or carbon of travel.

Definition: COIL (SUNY origin) & virtual exchange — co-taught, project-based online collaboration between geographically separated students.

  • BenefitsLow-cost, inclusive, scalable; reaches students who cannot travel; sustainable
  • ChallengesTime zones, digital access & equity, faculty design effort, depth vs. in-person immersion
  • GrowthAccelerated sharply after 2020
  • Best asA complement to — not replacement for — physical mobility
Student mobility

The flows of students across borders

Mobility is measured in two directions and two durations. Leaders design programs that maximize learning while managing cost, safety, and access.

Directions & durations

  • Inbound — international students hosted on the home campus
  • Outbound — home students who study abroad
  • Degree mobility — enrolling abroad for a full credential
  • Credit mobility — a term/year abroad counting toward the home degree (e.g. exchange, Erasmus+)
  • Short-term — faculty-led programs, summer, and field study, now the largest share of U.S. outbound

Global flows (general patterns, not precise stats)

  • Leading host countries — the United States, United Kingdom, Australia, Canada, Germany, and France attract large shares of internationally mobile students
  • Leading sending countries — China and India are the largest sources of internationally mobile students, followed by others across Asia and Africa
  • Emerging hubs — the Gulf states, China, and Malaysia host branch campuses and regional students
  • Flows shift with visa policy, currency, geopolitics, and post-study work rights

Leading a study-abroad program — the administrator's responsibilities

  • Program planning — learning outcomes, academic integration, credit approval, provider vetting
  • Health, safety & security — risk assessment, insurance, emergency & evacuation protocols, 24/7 contact
  • Risk management — travel warnings, duty of care, incident response, crisis communication
  • Equity & access — scholarships, credit-bearing options, advising to broaden who participates
  • Pre-departure & re-entry — orientation, cultural preparation, and support returning home
  • Compliance — Title IX abroad, Clery Act awareness, accessibility, and student conduct
Equity in education abroad is a persistent challenge: participants have historically skewed toward higher-income, majority-race, non-STEM, able-bodied students. Inclusive program design — funding, short-term and credit-bearing options, and targeted advising — is now a core leadership priority (see NAFSA and the Institute of International Education's efforts).
International student services

Supporting international students across the journey

Recruiting and enrolling international students is only the beginning. A comprehensive support system spans the full lifecycle from inquiry to alumni.

Recruitment & marketing

Reaching prospective students through agents, fairs, digital outreach, and partnerships — balancing enrollment goals with ethical recruitment and honest representation.

Admissions & credential evaluation

Assessing foreign transcripts and qualifications for equivalency (via evaluators such as NACES-member services or WES), plus English proficiency (TOEFL/IELTS) and holistic review.

Visas & immigration compliance

Issuing the Form I-20 (F-1) or DS-2019 (J-1), maintaining SEVIS records, and advising on status — the Designated School Official (DSO) role. Compliance protects the institution's ability to enroll international students.

Orientation & arrival

Immigration check-in, cultural adjustment, academic expectations, banking, housing, and health — front-loading the support that prevents later crises.

Ongoing support & belonging

Advising, counseling, mentoring, English support, and community — addressing culture shock, isolation, and academic-system differences.

Work authorization & transition

Advising on on-campus work, CPT and OPT (practical training), and post-study pathways — a decisive factor in destination choice.

SEVIS (the Student and Exchange Visitor Information System) is the U.S. federal database that tracks F and J visa holders. The DSO and Responsible Officer report enrollment, address, and status changes — making immigration advising a regulated, high-stakes function, not a clerical one. NAFSA: Association of International Educators is the leading professional home for this work.
Comparative systems & quality

How global higher education is structured and assured

Working across borders requires reading other systems — their degree structures, quality regimes, and the rankings that increasingly shape institutional behavior.

Bologna Process / EHEA

Convergence in Europe

Launched by the 1999 Bologna Declaration, the Bologna Process built the European Higher Education Area — harmonizing degree structures into a three-cycle system (bachelor's / master's / doctorate), the ECTS credit system, quality assurance standards, and the Diploma Supplement to make degrees comparable and portable across 40+ countries.

Degree structures

Systems differ in shape

  • Length and structure of the bachelor's degree vary (3 vs. 4 years)
  • Grading scales and credit systems are not equivalent (ECTS ≠ U.S. credit hours)
  • Binary systems separate research universities from applied/vocational institutions
  • Governance ranges from state-controlled to market-driven
Global rankings

Powerful — and contested

The major rankings — QS, Times Higher Education (THE), and the Academic Ranking of World Universities (ARWU / Shanghai) — shape reputation, recruitment, and policy.

Critiques: over-weight research over teaching; favor large, wealthy, English-language, science-heavy institutions; rely on reputation surveys and gameable metrics; narrow the definition of "quality"; and can distort institutional priorities.

Quality assurance

Trust across borders

Accreditation and quality assurance vary by country — from U.S. regional/programmatic accreditors to national QA agencies abroad. Cross-border collaboration relies on mutual recognition.

Key instruments: the INQAAHE network, the European ESG standards and ENQA, and UNESCO's Global Convention on the recognition of qualifications — all working to combat diploma mills and enable credential portability.

Equity & ethics

Whose knowledge, whose access, whose benefit?

A growing body of scholarship asks internationalization to confront its inequities and colonial legacies — and to build partnerships that are genuinely reciprocal.

Access & inclusion

  • Mobility and global learning skew toward the advantaged
  • "Internationalization at home" democratizes the benefits
  • Funding, credit-bearing options, and advising broaden participation
  • Disability, first-generation, and low-income access remain gaps

Decolonizing internationalization

  • Flows and prestige favor the Global North and English
  • Curricula and rankings privilege Western knowledge
  • Scholars (e.g. de Wit & Jones, Stein, Leask) urge critical, inclusive models
  • Center local knowledge systems and epistemic diversity

Sustainable partnerships

  • Reject "helicopter" research that extracts without benefit
  • Equitable authorship, funding, and capacity-building
  • Reciprocity over one-way recruitment or revenue flows
  • Environmental cost of mobility (carbon) enters the calculus
The reframing: leading scholars call for a shift from internationalization measured by activity counts and revenue toward "internationalization of higher education for society" — intentional, inclusive, ethical, and oriented to public good rather than prestige and profit.

Core vocabulary & scholars

  • Internationalization — integrating an international/intercultural/global dimension into teaching, research & service (Knight).
  • Internationalization at home — global learning for all students on the home campus (Beelen & Jones).
  • Comprehensive internationalization — institution-wide infusion of global perspective (Hudzik / ACE).
  • Credit vs. degree mobility — a term abroad for home credit vs. a full credential abroad.
  • COIL — Collaborative Online International Learning; a form of virtual exchange.
  • Bologna Process / EHEA — European harmonization of degree cycles & ECTS credits.
  • SEVIS — U.S. system tracking F/J visa holders; managed by the DSO.
  • Credential evaluation — assessing foreign qualifications for equivalency.
  • Branch campus — a physical presence abroad awarding the home credential.
  • Soft power — influence gained through educational & cultural attraction.
